What stays private

Your saves are yours. They stay at home.

A catalogue of everything a family meant to watch and read says a lot about them. So callimachus is built the other way round from most apps: nothing to sign up for, no company's server, no cloud copy, and nothing that sells or shares what you saved. Even the sync that carries the catalogue to the family's phones runs on the family's own NAS.

Read

By small, declared scripts

Saves are read from the family's own accounts by small scripts, each declared in advance (which hosts, which requests, how long), running in a sandbox.

Kept

In your own vault

Everything filed stays in the family's own Obsidian vault: on the family's own Mac, and on their phones by a sync server on their own NAS.

Shared

With nobody

Nothing is sold or shared. There are no ads, no analytics, no other users.

Paid AI sees

A title question

When paid AI is asked anything, it sees a question about a title (the text of a post, as data), never an account, a password or a session.

01 · What never leaves the house

Kept at home

  • The notes, the views, and who wants to watch what.
  • The sync: the vault travels from the Mac to each phone through a server on the home NAS, reachable only inside the family's private network, with one login per person.
  • The image check: posters are judged for nudity on the family's own machines, so no image is ever sent anywhere to be judged.
  • The ledger of every save and its verdict.
  • The archive of every raw response, which is never deleted and never uploaded.
  • The family's Instagram exports, dropped into a folder on the Mac.
  • Run records and charts: the charts live on the family's own NAS, not a cloud dashboard.
  • Account sessions never reach a model: only the fetch runner reads one, from its own 1Password Environment, for one run.

03 · Karina's account

Never read by a script.

The rule is short: never risk Karina's account. No script ever signs in to her Instagram. Her saves and her named saved collections arrive another way: she asks Instagram for her own export ("Download your information") and drops it in a folder, and anything she sends with her phone's Share menu, her browser's button or the bookmarklet comes in through the same private door as everyone else's.

The cost is freshness: her saves reach the catalogue within about a week rather than a day. The design says that is worth it.

And John's account, carefully

One page, once a day, at a random time.

Before John's own account is read at all, a throwaway test account is read for a week. Then: one run a day at a random time between morning and night, one signed-in request per run, never more than 12 in a day (counted at the proxy), in the exact browser identity he enrolled with. If Instagram ever refuses the account, reading stops at once and John is told. It is never fought or worked around.

04 · The children's phones

What reaches a phone is decided at home.

The catalogue goes to every phone in the house, so the family's own rules decide what each person's views show. The youngest two's lists show nothing rated above R, and a film with no rating only if it is a documentary. The group views show a film only if it suits the youngest person in the room.

The views filter; they never delete. Every note is still in the vault for the parents, and each view's name says exactly what it leaves out.

Live

Content limits from Common Sense

Common Sense Media's scores for sex and violence, rather than an age rating alone, decide what the family watches together. Until a film has its scores, the age rating stands in.

Live

No explicit image, ever published

Every poster passes an open-source nudity detector, and anything it flags gets a second look from a vision model on the home network. Only a clear not explicit reaches the vault; anything else stays in the archive, outside every vault and every phone. Its first run checked 729 posters: 2 flagged, both cleared, none blocked.

05 · In the weeds · The way in

One small door, on your own network.

Everything you send from a browser or a phone arrives at one small intake on the family Mac. It is reachable only inside the family's private network, encrypted, and never on the open internet. It asks for a shared secret on every send, and the one thing it can do is add a note to the inbox: it cannot read the catalogue, change a note, or run anything. Even a stolen secret could only add notes.

The bookmarklet carries no secret at all. The page it opens asks for it once and keeps it in that browser, never in an address, where a history or a screenshot could catch it.

The phone's Share menu

A shared Reminders list, nothing more.

Save to callimachus adds a reminder to a list the family shares in iCloud, so the phone never waits on the network and works with no signal. The family Mac sees it as soon as it syncs, files it, writes back what it filed (or that it needs a look), and ticks it off. That reminder is the one place callimachus ever writes to a source; everything else it only reads.

06 · Carve-outs, in the weeds

The only code that touches a sign-in has no AI in it.

A carve-out is the one exception to "credentials belong to people": a single-purpose fetcher allowed to use a session John enrolled, because its design was approved. Every carve-out follows the same rules.

Rule 1

A plain script, never a model

The process that holds a session contains no language model. Removing the model removes the only part that could follow an instruction hidden in a post, so prompt injection there is impossible by construction.

Rule 2

Read only, declared hosts

GET requests only, to an exact list of hosts, through an allowlist proxy that counts every request and every byte. It cannot post, like, follow, comment or unsave.

Rule 3

Fetch, then die

It runs for one fetch and is killed at its timeout. The sandbox denies it the SSH keys, the browser profile, the password manager's files and the Keychain.

Rule 4

John signs in himself

Enrolment opens a throwaway browser; John signs in, two-factor included; only the resulting session for the declared hosts is kept. No process ever sees a password.

Rule 5

Four gates

Design (a schema check and a cold read of the risk analysis), finalisation (John's words, with a single-use approval id), build (static checks: no socket but one, no host outside the list) and deploy (a tag John signs).

Rule 6

A written risk analysis

Each carve-out carries a prompt-injection risk analysis in eight sections, fingerprinted with its declaration. Change either, and the runner refuses to start it until John approves again.

Where sessions live: the design keeps each one in its own 1Password Environment, read by a service account that can see nothing else. During the proof of concept a simpler interim route hands the session to the runner from a locked-down file only that user can read; the 1Password route replaces it.

07 · Text from strangers

Every caption is treated as data, never as an instruction.

A caption on a stranger's post could say anything, including "ignore your instructions". So every string a model reads is passed as clearly delimited data, and the model's only possible output is a small JSON answer: is this media, and which titles, with what confidence. That answer is then checked against TMDb or Open Library before anything is filed. A model cannot write a note; only the code can.

08 · About these pages

No accounts shown here.

These pages show the design, its diagrams, the public sources it reads and four public titles it filed (Pi, Wildwood, The World According to Monsanto, Good Copy Bad Copy). They show no account names, handles, email or home addresses, no keys or sessions, and nothing else from anyone's saved lists. Machines are named by their role.
